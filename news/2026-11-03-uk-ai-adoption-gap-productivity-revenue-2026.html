32% of UK businesses use AI — only 12% report higher revenue. The adoption curve moved; the profit curve hasn't caught up

The DSIT 2026 survey shows AI adoption has doubled. But the same data reveals a stubborn gap: 75% report productivity gains, yet only 12% report higher revenue. Resultsense calls it the J-curve problem. Here is what it means and how to get on the right side of it.

3 NovPublished 2026

The UK government's DSIT AI Adoption Survey 2026 reports that 32% of UK businesses use AI — up from 16% a year ago. But a closer reading of the same data reveals an uncomfortable counterpoint: 75% of adopters report some productivity benefit, but only 12% report higher revenue. Only half describe their productivity gains as more than modest. 95% report no change in workforce size.

The J-curve problem

Analysis published by Resultsense on 3 November 2026 frames this as the classic J-curve dynamic: adoption went up sharply in 2026 while reported profit impact went the other way, at least temporarily. The J-curve analogy comes from business transformation literature — when organisations adopt a new capability, efficiency gains tend to appear first (doing the same things faster), while revenue impact requires process redesign and new capability deployment (doing things that weren't possible before). That second phase takes longer and requires deliberate investment.

The Productivity Institute's 2026 report, published in late October, reinforced the same pattern from a different angle: only 6% of UK firms report meaningful profit from AI despite 89% claiming some adoption. The numbers vary by survey methodology, but the pattern is consistent — AI adoption has run ahead of AI commercialisation.

Why the gap exists

The DSIT survey identifies the most common AI use case as administration (81% of those using or planning to use AI cite admin). Administration is the natural starting point — low risk, easy to test, immediate time savings. But administration is also the use case least likely to generate direct revenue impact. A business that automates its invoicing or meeting notes has saved time; it has not acquired a new customer or won a larger contract.

Revenue impact tends to come from AI deployed in customer-facing processes: faster quote responses that win more jobs, AI-assisted proposals that improve win rates, AI-powered lead follow-up that converts enquiries before competitors respond. These uses require more organisational change and trust in the tool than administration automation. They are also where competitive advantage compounds.

What separates the 12% from the 88%

Based on the survey data and additional reporting from Resultsense and the Productivity Institute, the businesses reporting revenue impact share a pattern: they rebuilt a complete process around AI rather than adding AI features to an unchanged process. The difference is not which AI tool is used — it is whether the workflow was redesigned to take advantage of what AI makes possible, not just what it makes faster.

A quote generation process redesigned around AI — where AI drafts the quote, automatically pulls in job history and pricing, and sends follow-ups at optimal times — produces faster quotes, fewer missed jobs, and better win rates. The same business adding AI spell-checking to manually drafted quotes produces slightly neater quotes and no revenue change.

Operator actions — getting on the right side of the curve

  • Audit where your AI is deployed: List every AI tool you use and which business process it supports. Separate tools that support internal efficiency from tools that directly touch revenue — customer acquisition, quote and proposal quality, customer retention. If all your AI is on the efficiency side, you have identified the gap.
  • Identify one revenue-facing process to redesign: Lead follow-up, quote generation, customer onboarding, or proposal writing. Pick the one with the highest revenue impact if improved. Redesign it from scratch with AI at the centre, not added on the side. The goal is a process that AI makes genuinely different, not just faster.
  • Measure revenue impact, not just time saved: Set a revenue metric for the redesigned process — quote win rate, lead response speed, customer conversion rate — and measure it before and after. Businesses that measure revenue impact tend to find it; businesses that only measure time saved tend to report modest productivity gains indefinitely.

Today's angle

The adoption headline is 32%. The harder number is 12% — the share that report higher revenue. The gap between those two numbers is the business opportunity. Getting on the right side of it requires redesigning one revenue-facing process around AI, not adding AI to existing processes.
