89% of businesses use AI — only 6% profit from it. Here is why the gap keeps growing

The Productivity Institute's October 2026 report commissioned by Bytes Technology Group finds organisations are adopting AI at speed but failing to redesign work around it. The result is impressive task-level demos and disappointing business-level returns.

89% of organisations now use AI somewhere in their business. Only 6% earn at least 5% of their profits from it. That gap — between broad adoption and meaningful return — is the central finding of the Productivity Institute's report published 29 October 2026.

The numbers behind the gap

Despite near-universal adoption, most staff in AI-using organisations spend just one to two hours per week on AI tasks. Only 25% of organisations have redesigned work processes around AI; 46% remain in pilot or experimental phases. AI is being installed without being integrated.

The individual performance improvements are striking: support staff completed 14% more queries with AI assistance; professionals worked 40% faster on writing and analysis tasks; programmers with AI tools tripled their code output. But at the business level, these gains collapse. The programming teams that tripled code output released only 30% more software, because testing and release bottlenecks were never changed to match the new pace of production.

The report calls this the J-curve problem: reorganising, retraining, and cleaning up data to support AI requires upfront cost and disruption, while the returns arrive later. Organisations that rush adoption without investing in the surrounding process changes often see a short-term productivity decline before gains appear, if they appear at all.

The management factor

The most striking finding for UK service businesses is the management variable. Firms rated as well-managed in 2020 were significantly more likely to be using AI effectively by 2023, suggesting that management practice explains around 80% of the difference between high and low AI adoption rates across similar industries. The implication is uncomfortable: AI does not fix weak management — it amplifies the distance between well-run and poorly-run organisations.

The report estimates that AI could contribute 0.5–0.7 percentage points annually to UK productivity growth, but only if businesses invest in parallel changes to management, training, and process design. Without that investment, AI spend becomes an expense line without a corresponding return line.

Coverage context

Report: The Productivity Institute, commissioned by Bytes Technology Group, published 29 October 2026. Statistics for task-level gains (support staff +14%, professionals +40%, programmers ×3) are drawn from referenced academic studies and pilot programme data cited in the report. The 6% profitability figure and 46% pilot-phase figure are from the same source. Management quality correlation is from longitudinal UK employer survey data referenced in the report.

Operator actions — closing your own gap

  • Audit where your team spends time with AI tools today — if it is less than two meaningful hours per person per week, adoption is in name only. Time spent is the leading indicator of eventual return.
  • Pick one process to redesign entirely, not just augment — adding AI to an existing workflow produces task-level gains that disappear at the bottleneck. Redesigning one complete workflow around AI produces business-level gains that compound.
  • Track output, not activity — count the invoices sent, the customer queries resolved, the quotes delivered, not the prompts written. That connects AI use to business outcomes you can defend on a P&L.
