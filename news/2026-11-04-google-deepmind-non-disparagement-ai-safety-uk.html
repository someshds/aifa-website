Google DeepMind UK staff push to end lifetime non-disparagement clauses — safety whistleblowing at the frontier

UK-based Google DeepMind employees are pressing executives to release them from non-disparagement clauses that bind them for life and prevent public warnings about AI risks. Google updated new hire terms but existing UK staff have only a verbal assurance. British lawmakers accused DeepMind of a "breach of trust" over a delayed safety report. What this signals for AI governance.

UK-based Google DeepMind researchers are pressing Google leadership to release them from non-disparagement clauses that prevent them from publicly warning about AI risks — even after they leave the company. The story surfaced after former DeepMind employee Sophia Liguori spoke to the BBC in October about Google's military AI development, triggering current UK staff to review their own contracts.

The non-disparagement clause problem at frontier AI labs

Non-disparagement clauses in employment contracts are common in UK commercial law. The standard clause prevents an employee from making public statements that damage the employer's reputation — during employment and typically for a fixed period after leaving. What emerged at Google DeepMind is unusual: the clauses are perpetual, binding researchers indefinitely after departure.

For safety researchers at a frontier AI lab, a perpetual non-disparagement clause creates a structural problem. A researcher who discovers a risk, escalates it internally, and is not satisfied with the response has essentially no external recourse. They cannot speak to regulators or journalists without breaching their contract and facing potential legal action — in perpetuity.

This is not unique to DeepMind. The same pattern emerged at OpenAI in 2024, when it became public that departing employees were asked to sign non-disparagement agreements that could result in the clawback of vested equity if they spoke publicly. That controversy contributed to the OpenAI governance reforms that followed. DeepMind is now facing the same scrutiny in the UK context.

Google's response and outstanding questions

Google has updated the terms for new hires to remove the post-termination aspect of the non-disparagement clause. A Google executive responded to the internal discussion on the company's internal messaging platform and said the company would not enforce the perpetual clause for workers who had already left.

The outstanding question is the form of that assurance. A verbal statement on an internal messaging platform is not a signed legal instrument that a former researcher could rely on. British lawmakers have reportedly characterised the situation as a "breach of trust" — the same legislators who previously questioned DeepMind over the delayed publication of the Gemini 2.5 Pro safety report.

The ICO's current agentic AI call for evidence (closing 20 November) covers safety disclosure obligations for AI developers — this legislative context is likely accelerating the internal pressure on Google to formalise its commitment.

What this means for UK businesses that use AI tools

The direct impact for most UK small businesses is limited. The DeepMind non-disparagement story is a governance and policy signal, not an operational one.

The indirect relevance is to AI vendor selection. When evaluating AI tools for business use, vendor safety practices — including whether safety researchers can raise concerns externally — are part of the risk picture. A lab that suppresses safety whistleblowing creates information asymmetry: you, the business customer, have less information about known model risks than you would have if internal concerns were allowed to surface.

Practically, UK business owners should ask AI vendors what their safety escalation policy looks like, and whether their published safety reports are complete and timely. The DeepMind/Gemini 2.5 Pro safety report delay (separately flagged by UK lawmakers) is an example of incomplete disclosure that affects vendor risk assessment.

Operator actions

  • No immediate action required — this is a governance signal, not an operational change. No AI tools are affected today.
  • When evaluating AI vendors — add "safety reporting policy" to your due diligence checklist. Ask if the vendor publishes safety evaluation reports, how frequently, and whether they have a disclosed process for escalating safety concerns externally.
  • If you have employees using AI tools with access to customer data — check that your AI use policy covers what to do if an employee identifies a data or safety concern with a tool. The ICO's guidance on this is in the agentic AI call for evidence documentation (ico.org.uk).
